# Inhabited observation room — revised scene plan

24 October 2026. Planning only. Supersedes the single-exhibit/tab composition in QUIET_SHIP_REVIEW.md and the equally illuminated three-exhibit concept in SPACESHIP_REDESIGN_PLAN.md. No application behavior changes in this turn.

## Design decision

One compact, inhabited spacecraft observation room contains every portfolio destination at once. The visitor looks around from an anchored position and selects meaningful objects. No project tabs, rotating product carousel, replacement exhibit, or required trip to another scene.

The previous extremes failed differently: the elaborate concept used too many equally bright focal points; the simplified build removed spatial interest and made exploration a tab switch. Solve the actual attention problem through light, scale, proximity, and stillness while preserving visible variety.

## Spatial concept: a place someone works

Build a curved observation cabin with a continuous, sculpted work surface following the panoramic window. Three projects occupy distinct sections of that surface; career, resume, and communication objects inhabit the side returns. A short upholstered reading bench, one plant, a folded textile, and a small task lamp can make it inhabited without becoming additional attractions. These are background details, not more hotspots.

Remove the central stepped display pedestal. Avoid a museum with three freestanding glowing monuments. The integrated surface has recessed supports, a few useful storage compartments, broad soft edges, material variation and convincing contact shadows. Its shape belongs to the hull rather than resembling a wooden office desk placed inside it.

Use an open central floor, a near foreground rail/seat edge, the work surface in the midground, then thick curved ribs, glazing, and a close planet. This layered composition supplies depth even when everything is still. The floor should not dominate the lower half of the opening frame.

Initial camera is a slightly offset three-quarter view rather than an exact symmetrical head-on shot. Compose the three projects at different depths and heights, all recognizable in the initial desktop view. Keep the central space open; let the work surface sweep naturally toward the sides.

## Attention hierarchy

1. Arrival: name/role, a convincing inhabited spacecraft, close planet, and clearly named real projects.
2. Exploration: a pointed-to or keyboard-focused object gets localized emphasis.
3. Reading: the chosen case study or career panel becomes the clear focus.

No three spotlights, animated project idles, permanent floating document clouds, or luminous outlines around every object. Similar materials unify the room, but silhouettes distinguish its contents. Most lighting is reflected daylight plus one warm practical source. Color accents identify small functional parts rather than flooding an entire installation.

Keep architecture medium contrast, projects slightly clearer, and the exterior framed behind them. The planet can be large without becoming the brightest element in every destination. Keep name and navigation readable in a quiet area instead of covering project models with a giant text gradient.

## Make the close planet feel close

The current small distant globe is inadequate. Enlarge and reframe Earth so the visible disc/limb occupies roughly 35–45% of the usable exterior window width in the opening desktop composition. Tune by actual screenshots, not arbitrary world coordinates. A partial disc can extend beyond the glazing, suggesting enormous scale; keep enough of its curvature and geography visible to recognize it.

Place the planet above and behind the work surface, with a useful clear band of exterior around it. Keep project silhouettes clear of its brightest limb. Surface texture, fine cloud structure and a restrained atmosphere give depth without a giant glowing halo. Reduce star prominence near the bright planet. Cast a subtle exterior color response onto window edges and upper material surfaces while preserving warm interior light.

Retain eight planets and the Sun in an optional navigation globe/destination tray. Sun exposure must preserve the readable room; Saturn's broad tilted ring plane can cross multiple window panes; Neptune gets localized exterior ice and edge frost. Do not turn each destination into a full-room color filter. Switching changes the exterior and gentle light response, not the room layout or camera position. Latest choice wins; loading failure retains a usable existing view.

## Intuitive discovery without a scavenger hunt

Three small HTML labels sit near the projects: real names plus a short function, for example 'NoClash / timetable planning'. They look like part of the room's identification system, not dashboard cards. Supporting object labels are restrained but explicit. Clamp labels to the viewport and hide occluded labels when necessary; do not let them stack over unrelated objects.

Objects share a consistent selection language: larger-than-visible hit area, pointer cursor, small material highlight or local lift on hover/focus, then a brief physical response on selection. Only the targeted object receives active emphasis. Decorative furniture has no fake interaction affordance.

Keep a quiet HTML header with Aayan Amir, the target role, Work, About, Resume and Contact. Work opens a scannable index of the three projects rather than project tabs in the scene. Native navigation is a shortcut to the exact same content. It must not imply a second required route through the portfolio.

Use one initial instruction: 'Drag to look. Select an object.' Retire the cue after interaction. No user manual, completion percentage, unlocked content or required hover discovery. Keyboard focus exposes the same labels/actions. Essential text does not depend on reading a tiny texture.

## Camera and reading behavior

Preserve the corrected look contract: drag right -> look right; drag up -> look up. Camera position remains anchored. Bounded yaw/pitch must allow the two side returns to be discovered without exposing unfinished hull geometry. Verify the complete permitted range during the hull blockout.

Looking around must not switch projects, reset the view, or trigger automatic centering. Selecting a project preserves the visitor's orientation; opening the reading panel pauses look input. Closing restores the same viewpoint. Reset is always available in View settings. No idle auto-pan, head bob, zoom jump, or forced fly-through.

On desktop, use a readable side panel with enough remaining view to retain the selected object and exterior. On small screens use a reading sheet with accessible close, focus management and internal scrolling. Preserve URL state, Back/Forward and reading scroll when changing destination. Optional motion is brief and interruptible; reduced motion shows the settled result immediately.

## Portrait composition

The same complete room and all its objects remain present. Do not reintroduce tabs or swap a single model for phone users. Use a dedicated portrait framing with the work surface arranged as a shallow crescent, allowing nearby objects and the next object along the curve to provide visible continuity. Gentle user-controlled rotation reveals the rest.

Where direct manipulation is inconvenient, show subtle native left/right look controls and a normal 'Work' overview. These steer the view or open the index; they do not unload or replace projects. Name, Resume and Contact remain easy to reach. Avoid showing six overlapping labels at once. Review narrow widths and zoom as actual rendered layouts, and distinguish viewport checks from physical phone GPU testing.

## Visual language and detail budget

Palette: soft ceramic #CBD2CF, brushed titanium #83929A, charcoal blue #233742, muted upholstery #657B78, warm lamp #D7B484, exterior #050D19. Use ice-blue only on small functional details. No gold-trim palace or polished chrome everywhere.

Keep existing readable type families initially. Hierarchy and placement matter more than changing fonts again. Make the name confident and comfortably readable; avoid a giant slogan competing with the room.

Spend geometry on hull curvature, work-surface profile and project silhouettes. Add a few believable joints, grips, fasteners, insets and fabric/roughness details that explain construction. Avoid arbitrary greebles and repeated seams covering every surface. A plant, lamp and notebook should make the room feel used, not form another row of collectible icons.

Lighting should establish: a soft key, gentle shadow gradients, contact beneath objects, a broad reflected window light, and a localized warm reading nook. The unprocessed render must work before adding restrained ambient occlusion or bloom. No depth-of-field blur on usable objects or HTML.

## Technical and performance plan

Retain Astro, React Three Fiber, project content and validated navigation. Replace the active-featured-model state and tab UI with a stable registry of object identities, transforms and content actions. One shared selection controller handles pointer, keyboard and HTML navigation.

Separate modules: CabinShell, IntegratedWorkSurface, ProjectObjects, PersonalObjects, LightingRig, AnchoredLookController, DestinationBackdrop and PortfolioOverlay. All objects stay mounted; only selected/hovered visual state changes. Reuse shared geometry/materials and instance repeated details. Restrict shadow casters. Load at most needed destination resources with explicit cleanup.

The scene should settle to demand rendering. No continuous decorative animation. A selected animation invalidates frames only until it settles; hidden tabs and reduced motion stop unnecessary work. Low-quality mode reduces shadow/effect resolution and peripheral detail while preserving meaningful objects and layout.

Measure frame-time percentiles, calls, triangles, memory trends, idle rendering and cold load on available hardware. Establish the all-object baseline before adding effects. Current one-exhibit sample is not a valid benchmark for the richer room. Do not sacrifice defining silhouettes merely to hit an arbitrary triangle count, and do not treat a passing build as visual validation.

## Build stages and gates

1. Spatial blockout: close planet, curved work surface, all three project silhouettes, career notebook, resume folio and contact radio. Initial desktop view and full permitted camera sweep. No effects expansion yet.
2. Composition review: actual desktop and portrait renders must read as inhabited, calm and interesting. All three projects remain spatially discoverable without tabs. Refine layout before detail.
3. Materials and lighting: ceramic/metal/fabric differentiation, believable contacts, a warm nook, restrained glass and planet illumination. Compare stills without active highlights.
4. Interactions: meaningful object actions, reliable hit areas, labels, native shortcuts, readable panels, resume handling, camera continuity, keyboard and reduced motion.
5. Destinations and finishing: planetary scale, transition, Sun/Saturn/Neptune treatments, ambient detail and quality modes.
6. Verification: navigation/history, resource cycles, idle frames, phone layouts, error fallback, performance and production checks.

Acceptance questions:
- Does the still image feel like a place someone occupies, rather than an empty display stage?
- Can the visitor identify three different real projects without switching a tab?
- Is there enough visual variety to reward looking around, without every surface glowing or moving?
- Is the planet unmistakably close and visible across the window?
- Are career information, resume and contact obvious and reachable in this same scene?
- Does a quick recruiter visit work just as well as a leisurely exploration?

UX lenses: Law of Proximity groups projects along one surface; Similarity makes actions recognizable; Fitts's Law supports generous object targets; Hick's Law keeps scene choices clear and destinations optional; Aesthetic-Usability requires a visual review separately from functional testing.
